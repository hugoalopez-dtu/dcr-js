\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter presents the experimental results obtained from the two
benchmark evaluations described in Chapter~\ref{chap:experimental_setup}.
The results are organised to address the four evaluation questions
defined in Section~\ref{sec:experimental_objectives}.
Section~\ref{sec:expense_results} reports on the Expense Report benchmark,
covering Pareto recovery correctness and compliance behaviour
(\textbf{EQ1} and \textbf{EQ2}).
Section~\ref{sec:loanapp_results} reports on the role-conditioned Loan
Application benchmark, evaluating scalability and comparative positioning
against prior work (\textbf{EQ3} and \textbf{EQ4}).
Section~\ref{sec:results_summary} summarises the main findings across
both experiments.

% ============================================================
\section{Exact Pareto Recovery: Expense Report}
\label{sec:expense_results}

This section addresses \textbf{EQ1} (correctness) and
\textbf{EQ2} (compliance guarantee). The Expense Report
benchmark provides a controlled setting in which the exact
Pareto front is known from an independent CSP+COP solver,
enabling a direct correctness comparison between the proposed
\ac{RL} framework and an exact optimisation method.

\subsection{Ground Truth and Baseline Comparison}
\label{sec:expense_baseline}

As ground truth, the CSP+COP solver was executed on the Expense Report
\ac{DCR} graph with maximum trace length $k=5$, minimising both cost and duration simultaneously.
The solver identifies two Pareto-optimal traces in 1.01 seconds
(Table~\ref{tab:expense_pareto_csp}).

Following Diaz et al. \cite{Diaz2024Pareto-OptimalModels}, cost values are expressed in euros (€) and duration in minutes (min); these are the only experiments in this thesis where concrete units are assigned to the objective dimensions.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Exact Pareto front obtained with
         CSP+COP~\cite{Diaz2024Pareto-OptimalModels}.}
\label{tab:expense_pareto_csp}
\begin{tabular}{lrrl}
\toprule
\textbf{Point} & \textbf{Cost (€)} & \textbf{Duration (min)} & \textbf{Trace} \\
\midrule
P1 & 50  & 10{,}500 & FillOut $\rightarrow$ Approve $\rightarrow$ PayOut \\
P2 & 710 & 2{,}700  & FillOut $\rightarrow$ Withdraw \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

A PPO agent was trained using a normalised reward formulation
(Section~\ref{sec:reward_normalised}).
Under this formulation the agent recovers both Pareto-optimal points, matching the exact solver output.

Reward normalisation is essential for stable learning in this setting. Without it, the raw penalty for a single high-cost action dominates the episodic signal, a five-step trace at maximum cost and duration yields a cumulative reward on the order of $-1{,}500$, while the structural completion bonus is at most $+100$. Under such magnitudes the cost/duration gradient overwhelms the signal that guides the agent toward accepting states. Normalising each objective to $[0,1]$ before scalarisation ensures that structural and economic reward components remain commensurable throughout training.

The reward convergence curve under the normalised formulation is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:reward_convergence}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Pictures/Expense_Report_reward_convergence.png}
    \caption{Reward convergence across weight configurations after
             reward normalisation.}
    \label{fig:reward_convergence}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Compliance and Illegal-Action Ratio}
\label{sec:expense_compliance}

The shielding mechanism operates at the environment level: any action not enabled under the current \ac{DCR} marking is intercepted before execution, leaving the process state unchanged and returning a fixed
penalty. This guarantee holds throughout training, the process never enters an invalid state regardless of agent behaviour.
The illegal-action ratio therefore measures how frequently the agent \emph{attempted} blocked actions, and reflects learning dynamics rather than any compliance violation.

Across the full training run, between 4.4\% and 8.7\% of all steps are illegal action attempts, a ratio that reflects early exploration of the 24-action space rather than systematic non-compliance. By the final 20\% of training this figure drops to below 10\% in all weight configurations, and below 1\% in four of the six (Table~\ref{tab:illegal_actions}).


With the normalised reward, this ratio remains below 15\% in the final 20\% of training across all six weight configurations, and the acceptance rate reaches 100\% in all cases (Table~\ref{tab:illegal_actions}).

($\alpha=1.0,\,\beta=0.0$ and $\alpha=0.0,\,\beta=1.0$) penalise a
single objective most aggressively, which reduces the relative
attractiveness of certain legal actions and encourages broader
exploration early in training.
Despite this, both configurations converge to 100\% acceptance,
confirming that normalisation is sufficient to make legal behaviour
the dominant strategy.

\section{Loan Application: Scalability and Role-Conditioned Trade-offs}
\label{sec:results_loanapp}

%% CAMBIO 2: párrafo de apertura reescrito -- ya no dice
%% "the purpose here is external". Se describe como extensión de
%% escala/riqueza, y se referencia explícitamente que la validación
%% de mecanismo (shield vs no-shield) vive en el Cap. 7, mientras que
%% la validez frente a estructura nunca vista vive en el Cap. 8.
This section evaluates the full framework on the role-conditioned Loan
Application benchmark. The purpose here is to extend the scale and
richness of the evaluation beyond the five-event Expense Report case:
to assess whether the scalarised reward produces meaningful
cost--duration trade-offs in a larger, role-conditioned action space,
and what role-conditioned allocation adds once resource scarcity is
introduced. This is the same benchmark used in
Chapter~\ref{chap:internal_validation} to isolate the contribution of
shielding and learning (EQ5); the two chapters analyse it from
different angles rather than duplicating the same question. Whether
this benchmark's findings generalise to process structures never seen
during development is a separate question, addressed independently on
the mined-graph benchmark grid of Chapter~\ref{chap:external_validation}.
Compliance behaviour and its ablation are not re-analysed here, since
that mechanism-level question is the subject of
Chapter~\ref{chap:internal_validation}; the full-framework Pareto front
characterised below is the same one that appears there as the Safe
\ac{RL} arm of the ablation. For completeness,
Appendix~\ref{app:loanapp_diagnostics} reports the omitted Loan
Application diagnostics: reward convergence
(Figure~\ref{fig:app_loanapp_reward}), accept rate
(Figure~\ref{fig:app_loanapp_accept}), shielding activity
(Figure~\ref{fig:app_loanapp_shielding}), and clean accepting episodes
(Figure~\ref{fig:app_loanapp_clean}).

The Loan Application process contains 12 events. With two available
role profiles, Junior and Expert, the action space expands to 24
event--role actions. The observation remains the encoded \ac{DCR}
marking, while role choice affects only the cost and duration incurred
by an executed event.

\paragraph{Two levels of resource modelling.}
The evaluation proceeds in two levels, and it is important to delimit
the claim of each. The first level treats Junior and Expert as
always-available role profiles that scale event cost and duration. Under
unlimited availability the weight-optimal role of each event is
independent of the others, so role selection is separable across events
and does not, on its own, constitute resource allocation. The second
level introduces scarcity through a per-case Expert capacity budget,
which couples the role decisions and turns role selection into an
allocation problem under opportunity cost. Neither level models
inter-case contention, queues, waiting time, or utilisation; the
comparison with simulation-based approaches such as Kirchdorfer et
al.~\cite{KirchdorferLukasFromPolicies} and
Doumeni~\cite{ArtemisDoumeniParetoAgentSimulator} therefore remains a
comparison of trade-off structure rather than of absolute figures.

\subsection{Pareto Front Recovery (Unlimited Capacity)}
\label{sec:loanapp_pareto}

The Pareto front was extracted from accepting traces generated during
the final 20\,\% of training, with both roles always available. As in
the other Pareto analyses, the unweighted baseline $(\alpha=0,\beta=0)$
is excluded from the dominance computation, since it does not express a
cost--duration preference. The complete list of Pareto-optimal points is
provided in Appendix~\ref{ap:loanapp_pareto_points}
(Table~\ref{tab:app_loanapp_pareto_points}).

Figure~\ref{fig:loanapp_pareto_balanced} shows the recovered front. The
agent finds 26 non-dominated cost--duration points, spanning a cost
range from 1\,092 to 3\,120 and a duration range from 408 to 1\,632.
The black stars denote Pareto-optimal accepting traces recovered by the
full framework. The orange diamond marks the most balanced point on the
front, selected as the solution closest to the ideal
minimum-cost/minimum-duration corner after min--max normalising both
objectives. This criterion follows the same interpretation used by
Kirchdorfer et al.~\cite{KirchdorferLukasFromPolicies}: rather than
choosing one extreme, it identifies the compromise point with the
smallest joint distance to the best observed value of each objective.

The minimum-cost point was independently checked against the exact
CSP+COP solver: with role choice removed (flat, single-valued
cost/duration per event), the solver's unconstrained optimum is
cost\,=\,1\,560, duration\,=\,816 over the same five events
(\textit{Check application form completeness}, \textit{Appraise
property}, \textit{Check credit history}, \textit{AML check},
\textit{Assess loan risk}), reached in 5.6\,s and proven optimal. Once
both figures are rescaled by the Junior role multiplier
($\times0.7$ cost, $\times2.0$ duration), they match the recovered
minimum-cost point exactly (1\,092.0, 1\,632.0), confirming this
extreme of the front is the true global optimum rather than an
artefact of training.

weights steer the trained policies towards different regions of the
trade-off surface. The cost-focused configuration reaches the cheapest
valid executions, the duration-focused configuration reaches the
fastest valid executions, and the balanced configuration contributes a
central compromise. A notable pattern, visible in the complete table in
Appendix~\ref{app:loanapp_pareto_points}, is that many intermediate
Pareto points are produced by the balanced configuration
$(\alpha=0.5,\beta=0.5)$ rather than by the two extreme configurations.
This suggests that balanced rewards maintain broader exploration of the
valid trace space, whereas specialised objectives converge more quickly
towards one end of the front. This behaviour is revisited in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion_reward}.

\subsection{Role Specialisation Patterns}
\label{sec:loanapp_roles}

Beyond the aggregate cost--duration trade-off, the role-conditioned
action space lets the policy express \emph{which} events it staffs with
each role, and how that choice responds to the objective weights.
Figure~\ref{fig:loanapp_role_slopes} reports the Expert-assignment rate
per event under the cost-focused $(\alpha{=}1,\beta{=}0)$ and
duration-focused $(\alpha{=}0,\beta{=}1)$ regimes; the slope of each
line measures how strongly an event's staffing responds to the
objective.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Pictures/LoanApp_role_slopegraph.png}
  \caption{Expert-assignment rate per event under the cost-focused and
  duration-focused regimes. Each line is one event; a steep slope
  indicates strong sensitivity of the staffing decision to the
  objective, a flat line indicates a role-indifferent activity.}
  \label{fig:loanapp_role_slopes}
\end{figure}

Three qualitative categories emerge and persist across the full weight
sweep (Appendix~\ref{app:loanapp_role_distribution}). High-sensitivity
events such as \emph{AML check} and \emph{Appraise property} swing
sharply between Junior and Expert dominance as the objective changes:
their high base cost and duration produce the largest reward
differential between roles, and therefore the strongest policy gradient.
Lower-impact events respond weakly. \emph{Assess loan risk} is a
boundary case, remaining close to a balanced 50\,\% Expert assignment
irrespective of the weights, because its normalised cost--duration
profile lies near the indifference threshold of the reward.

This event-level view is the granularity the framework adds over pool-
or policy-level resource approaches: it identifies not only that a
cost--duration trade-off exists, but precisely which activities drive it
and which are staffing-neutral. From a process-management standpoint,
role-indifferent activities can be staffed on availability grounds with
no measurable trade-off impact, while optimisation effort concentrates
on the high-sensitivity events. The mechanism behind the three
categories is analysed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion_roles}.

\paragraph{Stability across seeds.}
To confirm that these categories are not an artefact of a single
training run, the role-conditioned experiment was repeated under $n=4$
random seeds. At the front level, the recovered Pareto set is highly
stable: $26.25 \pm 0.5$ points across seeds (Figure~\ref{fig:loanapp_seed_pareto_overlay}),
with near-identical illegal-action rates ($1.13\% \pm 0.34\%$), mean cost
at the cost-focused pole ($1\,503 \pm 65$), and mean duration at the
duration-focused pole ($657 \pm 48$).

At the per-event role-assignment level, stability is uneven across the
three categories identified in Section~\ref{sec:discussion_roles}. The
high-sensitivity events (AML check, Appraise property) and the
role-indifferent event (Assess loan risk) are robust, varying by only
3--9 percentage points in Expert-assignment rate across seeds. The
moderate-response events (Check application form completeness, Check
credit history, Return application back to applicant), by contrast,
vary by 18--32 percentage points across seeds
(Figure~\ref{fig:loanapp_seed_illegal_boxplot}). This is consistent with
their cost--duration profiles sitting closer to the reward's
indifference threshold than the high-sensitivity events: the gradient
signal distinguishing Junior from Expert is weaker for these events, so
the specific role split the policy converges to is more sensitive to
the stochastic exploration of a given run. The qualitative three-category
structure itself, however, replicates across all four seeds.

\subsection{Capacity-constrained Role Allocation}
\label{sec:loanapp_capacity}

The analysis so far assumes both roles are always available, under which
role selection is separable across events: each event independently
takes its weight-optimal role, and no decision constrains any other.
This section removes that assumption by limiting the Expert role to a
per-case budget of $k$ assignments, as defined in
Section~\ref{sec:loan_dataset}. A finite budget couples the decisions,
so the policy must allocate a scarce resource rather than apply a
per-event rule.

The constraint and the optimisation are kept separate. The budget limit
is enforced by the same shield that enforces \ac{DCR} enablement: an
Expert action with no remaining budget is intercepted without modifying
the marking, so the compliance guarantee of contribution~C1 extends
unchanged to the capacity constraint. The shield, however, only blocks;
it does not decide where the budget is spent. That decision is learned.
The remaining budget is part of the observation, so the policy can
condition on it, and the reward signal teaches the agent that spending
the scarce Expert early on a low-impact event leaves none for a
high-impact one later. Allocating the budget well is therefore a
sequential credit-assignment problem of the same kind the agent solves
when recovering the Pareto front, and one that a myopic per-event
heuristic cannot address by construction.

\paragraph{Relation to Kirchdorfer et al.'s evaluation design.}
Because the Loan Application costs are synthetic and the metrics and
granularity differ from prior work, the cost and duration figures
recovered here are not numerically comparable to those of Kirchdorfer et
al.~\cite{KirchdorferLukasFromPolicies} or
Doumeni~\cite{ArtemisDoumeniParetoAgentSimulator}; that comparison
remains qualitative and is developed in Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}.
This experiment is best understood as a methodological replication
rather than as external validation in the sense of
Chapter~\ref{chap:external_validation}: it reproduces the evaluation
design of prior resource-allocation work, in which an optimised policy
is benchmarked against standard allocation heuristics, and applies it
independently on a \ac{DCR}-shielded learning agent, on the same
internal benchmark used throughout this chapter and
Chapter~\ref{chap:internal_validation}. The heuristics used here,
Always-Junior, Always-Expert, and greedy-per-event, are the same family
of cheapest, fastest, and myopic-greedy references that Kirchdorfer et
al.\ benchmark against. The question is therefore not whether the
recovered numbers match theirs, but whether the same qualitative
conclusion holds, namely that a learned allocation policy improves on
naive heuristics under scarcity.

The experiment is organised around three questions:
\begin{description}
  \item[Q1.] How does scarcity reshape the recovered front relative to
  the unconstrained case of Section~\ref{sec:loanapp_pareto}?
  \item[Q2.] When Expert capacity is scarce, does the learned policy
  concentrate it on the high-sensitivity events identified in
  Section~\ref{sec:loanapp_roles}?
  \item[Q3.] Does the learned policy improve on the standard allocation
  heuristics, as an independent replication of the
  optimised-policy-versus-heuristics comparison reported by Kirchdorfer
  et al.?
\end{description}

them first whenever capacity allows. Second, an indifferent event

regardless of $k$, including under the most extreme scarcity ---
stronger than the boundary behaviour of Assess loan risk, which is
indifferent only when capacity is unlimited and collapses to 6\% under

opposite of the monotonic pattern: under extreme scarcity ($k=1$) it
absorbs a disproportionate 70\% of the available budget, a priority that
dilutes as capacity grows (70\%$\to$61\%$\to$52\%$\to$41\%). This third
pattern was not anticipated and indicates that scarcity does not simply
scale down the unconstrained allocation uniformly --- it changes which
events the policy treats as priorities.

Three findings follow. First, the learned policy extends the recovered
front further than the greedy heuristic at every budget: the two track
each other closely in the region where both operate
(Figure~\ref{fig:q3_heuristics_k2}), but the learned policy alone
reaches the cheapest and the most duration-efficient extremes, recovering
the qualitative conclusion of Kirchdorfer et al.\ from a different
paradigm while also retaining the compliance guarantee of
Section~\ref{sec:shield_contribution}, which neither heuristic provides
by construction. Second, both non-weight-conditioned heuristics are far
worse than either optimised approach at every budget (median duration
above 4\,000 against a learned-policy maximum of 1\,632), confirming
that ignoring cost and duration entirely is costly even when
feasibility is not at stake. Third, and more interesting than either:
Always-Expert is not meaningfully better than Always-Junior, despite
having access to a resource Always-Junior never uses. At $k=2$, for
instance, Always-Expert trades a higher median cost (3\,541 against
3\,126) for only a modest reduction in duration (4\,380 against
4\,683) --- a far smaller return than the same budget yields under
Greedy or the learned policy, which reach substantially lower duration
at comparable or lower cost. Spending the scarce Expert role on
whichever event happens to be selected first, without regard to which
events the process most benefits from it, captures almost none of the
value the resource could provide: the budget's value lies almost
entirely in \emph{where} it is spent, not merely in having access to
it, and a fixed rule with no notion of cost or duration cannot exploit
that.

\subsection{Interpretation and Scope}
\label{sec:loanapp_interpretation}

The Loan Application experiment provides three pieces of evidence on
top of the Expense Report case. First, the framework scales from the
five-event Expense Report benchmark to a larger 12-event process with a
24-action role-conditioned action space. Second, the recovered front
exhibits the expected economic structure: reducing duration requires
accepting higher cost, and reducing cost leads to longer executions.
Third, once the Expert role is made scarce, the framework expresses an
allocation decision rather than a per-event rule, and the same shield
that guarantees \ac{DCR} compliance also enforces the capacity limit.

This trade-off structure is qualitatively consistent with
resource-aware process optimisation studies such as Kirchdorfer et
al.~\cite{KirchdorferLukasFromPolicies}, where faster or more capable
resources reduce temporal metrics at higher cost. The comparison should
be interpreted at the level of trade-off structure, not as a direct
resource-allocation benchmark. The scope of the present model is
bounded in two ways. In the unconstrained case, Junior and Expert are
role profiles that scale event cost and duration, not individual
resources, so role selection is separable across events. In the
capacity-constrained case, scarcity couples the decisions within a case
and yields opportunity-cost allocation, but the model still represents
neither inter-case contention nor queues, waiting time, or utilisation.
Full operational resource allocation, with shared resources across
concurrent cases and stateful availability, would require a multi-case
environment and is treated as future work in
Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}.

Whether the scalability and role-conditioned behaviour established here
hold beyond this single benchmark, on process structures never touched
during the development of the method, is addressed independently in
Chapter~\ref{chap:external_validation}.
